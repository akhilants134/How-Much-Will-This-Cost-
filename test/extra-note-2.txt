The human immune system is a complex network of cells, tissues, and organs that work together to defend the body against pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. It is broadly divided into two main branches: the innate immune system and the adaptive immune system.

The innate immune system is the body's first line of defence. It responds rapidly and non-specifically to any foreign invader. Physical barriers like skin and mucous membranes prevent pathogens from entering. When a pathogen does breach these barriers, innate immune cells such as macrophages, neutrophils, and natural killer (NK) cells are immediately activated. These cells identify pathogens through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that detect conserved molecular structures found on microbes, called pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs). Inflammation is a hallmark response of the innate system — it increases blood flow and recruits more immune cells to the infection site.

The adaptive immune system, in contrast, is slower to respond but highly specific. It involves two major types of lymphocytes: B cells and T cells. B cells produce antibodies — proteins that bind specifically to antigens on the surface of pathogens, marking them for destruction. T cells come in two main types: helper T cells (CD4+), which coordinate the immune response by activating B cells and cytotoxic T cells, and cytotoxic T cells (CD8+), which directly kill infected cells.

A critical feature of the adaptive immune system is immunological memory. After an initial infection, memory B and T cells remain in the body long-term. Upon re-exposure to the same pathogen, this memory allows for a faster and stronger response, often preventing illness entirely. This principle underlies vaccination — exposing the immune system to a harmless form or component of a pathogen to build memory without causing disease.

Autoimmune diseases occur when the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's own tissues, as in rheumatoid arthritis or type 1 diabetes. Immunodeficiency disorders, like HIV/AIDS, impair immune function, leaving individuals vulnerable to opportunistic infections.
